# A repair workshop with no mandatory phone

**Illustrative design case.** This is an authored example of propositions changing one another, not a transcript of real participants, evidence of native chat execution or a validated community service.

The brief is to design a neighborhood repair afternoon: visitors bring a broken household item, volunteers help them understand a possible repair, and nobody needs a smartphone to participate. There is one room and limited volunteer time. Attendance, skill mix and available tools are initially unknown.

Taylor's independent opening reframes a conventional booking form as an “object passport”: a small card travels with the item, preserves what was tried and helps the visitor learn. Morgan sees a progression from arrival to diagnosis to a next action. Alex foregrounds the volunteer who must route unfamiliar repairs while the room gets noisy. Calvin asks whether throughput estimates wrongly treat every item as equally difficult. Nathan asks whether the design leaves a repeatable practice or an exhausting one-off event.

Taylor initially suggests a digital display showing each passport's position. Alex challenges the hidden operating burden: someone has to update it while volunteers have their hands full. Calvin adds that a queue number gives false precision when a five-minute lamp check and an uncertain sewing repair share the same line. The objections change Taylor's proposal: passports become physical cards routed into visible “first look,” “working together” and “next step” trays, each tied to an actual volunteer station.

Morgan integrates the trays with a short welcoming conversation and asks whether visitors can leave with something useful when no repair is possible. Taylor proposes a small diagram or annotated photo; Alex points out that photos are unavailable for some visitors and unnecessary for many items. The design now uses a plain card with the observed issue, what was tried, the next possible action and any uncertainty. A photo is optional. Calvin asks the team to distinguish an observed symptom from a diagnosis so the artifact does not acquire false authority.

Nathan changes the proposed first event from a general repair fair to a bounded afternoon around the volunteers' confirmed skills, with explicit out-of-scope items and a record of unmet demand. Taylor argues for preserving discovery rather than making the invitation joyless. Morgan joins the two: an inviting public description with specific supported categories and a welcome route for learning what help is possible. The eventual invitation would require actual publication authority; designing it does not send it.

The final conditional design is an arrival-to-next-action journey with physical passports, three meaningful work states, a short triage conversation and a take-home record. Its first experiment is a tabletop rehearsal with representative varied items and the real volunteers. The observation that matters is whether people can understand their next action and whether work gets stranded without an owner. No invented throughput number is needed to select that experiment.

A real CONCEIVE would retain five actual IDs and their own initial views, actual challenge messages, the changed artifacts and final revision responses. Its handoff would state unknown attendance and tools, confirm the volunteer scope before an event commitment, and give BRAID the authorized making work only if the human asked to build or run it. The useful outcome here is a changed, more situated design—not five impressive monologues.
